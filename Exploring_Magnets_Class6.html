1 · Definition

What is a magnet?

  • A magnet is an object that attracts certain metals toward itself.
  • It attracts iron, nickel, cobalt and steel.
  • Steel is attracted because it contains iron.

Watch: the iron nail and steel spoon get pulled. The wood and plastic don't move.

5 · Poles

Every magnet has two poles

  • North pole (N) and South pole (S) – the two ends.
  • Pull is strongest at the poles – iron filings crowd at the ends, not the middle.
  • Poles always come in pairs. Cut a magnet – each piece gets its own N and S!

6A · Directive property

Magnets find directions

  • A freely hanging bar magnet always rests in the North–South direction.
  • The end pointing north is the North pole; the other end is the South pole.
  • Why? The Earth itself is a giant magnet.
  • A compass uses this – a tiny magnetic needle that spins freely and settles pointing north.
6B · Attraction and repulsion

Unlike poles attract, like poles repel

NS
NS
  • Unlike poles attract: N ↔ S pull toward each other.
  • Like poles repel: N ← → N or S ← → S push each other away.
6C · Magnetic effect

Magnetism passes through things

  • A magnet's pull can act through non-magnetic materials.
  • Paper, cardboard and thin wooden planks do not block it.
  • Try it: move a magnet under a sheet of paper – a paper clip on top follows it!

7 · Make your own magnet

Magnetization

  1. Place a steel needle flat on a wooden table.
  2. Put one pole of a bar magnet on one end of the needle.
  3. Stroke the magnet along the needle in one direction only.
  4. Lift it at the far end, bring it back to the start, and stroke again.
  5. Repeat 30 to 50 times – the needle becomes a magnet!

8 · Care and storage

Keep your magnets strong

❌ Magnets lose their power if you…

  • Heat them over a flame
  • Hammer or hit them with hard objects
  • Drop them again and again from a height
  • Keep them next to phones, TVs or remote controls

✅ Store them properly

  • Keep bar magnets in pairs, opposite poles side by side
  • Put a piece of wood or plastic between them
  • Place soft iron keepers across the ends

Question 2

True or False?

(i) A magnet can be broken into pieces to obtain a single pole.

False – poles always exist in pairs. Every piece gets its own N and S.

(ii) Similar poles of a magnet repel each other.

True – like poles repel, unlike poles attract.

(iii) Iron filings mostly stick in the middle of a bar magnet when it is brought near them.

False – most filings stick near the ends (poles), where the magnet is strongest.

(iv) A freely suspended bar magnet always aligns with the north-south direction.

True – this is the directive property of a magnet.

Question 7

Find the North pole without another magnet

The bar magnet has no markings and you have no second magnet. How do you find its North pole?

Tie a thread to the middle of the magnet and suspend it so it can turn freely. When it comes to rest, the end pointing towards geographic North is its North pole.

Question 8

Where are Earth's magnetic poles?

If Earth is a magnet, can we guess its poles by looking at a compass?

Yes! The N pole of every compass needle points to geographic North. Unlike poles attract, so the magnetic pole near geographic North must be a magnetic South pole, and the one near geographic South is a magnetic North pole.

Question 9

The mechanic's falling screws

While repairing a gadget, the steel screws keep falling off the screwdriver. How can the mechanic fix this?

Rub the tip of the screwdriver with a permanent magnet to magnetise it. The magnetised tip holds the steel screws by magnetic attraction, so they don't fall.

Question 10

The floating ring magnet

Ring magnet X stays floating above ring magnet Y on a wooden stand. Why? How can we make X touch Y without pushing either magnet?

Reason: the facing surfaces of X and Y are like poles, so they repel.

Solution: remove X, turn it upside down and slide it back. Now unlike poles face each other, so X is attracted and drops down to touch Y.
